The Waiting Room

Everybody knows black dogs wait longer.
347,587 shelter records disagree.

Black dog syndrome is the best-known piece of folklore in animal rescue: dark-coated dogs are said to sit in shelters far longer than light ones. Shelters repeat it themselves, and it is why some of them run black-dog promotions. So I loaded every intake and outcome Austin Animal Center has published — 347,587 rows — into a columnar warehouse and measured it.

The median black dog is adopted in 9 days. Every other colour: 9 days. The gap is nothing. But something real is hiding underneath it.

1. The folklore, measured

Here is every coat colour with at least 300 adoptions, ranked by how long the median dog waited. If the myth were true, black would be at the top. It sits in the middle of the pack, level with white — and it is the single largest group in the data.

2. But one colour really does wait

Blue dogs wait 21 days — more than twice the median. “Blue” is a grey-slate coat, and if you know dogs you can already guess the problem. Plot each colour against the share of dogs wearing it that the shelter recorded as a pit bull, and the whole ranking resolves into one line.

Blue is 70% pit bull — the highest of any colour. Black is just 9.5%. The colour ranking was never about colour.

3. Control for breed and the colour effect disappears

The test: hold breed fixed and let colour vary. Among dogs that are not pit bulls, the spread across every coat colour collapses to a few days. Among pit bulls, every colour waits a long time. Colour is not doing the work in either group.

There is one honest caveat, and it is the interesting part. Inside the pit bull group, black is the slowest colour — 31 days against about 26 for the rest. So black dog syndrome is not invented. It is a roughly five-day effect living inside a nineteen-day breed effect, and the folklore credits it with the whole gap.

4. What actually decides how long a dog waits

Breed, and age. Both effects are about three times larger than anything colour does.

5. So write the listing against the real problem

Knowing this changes what a shelter should write. The instinct for a black dog is “don’t overlook me because of my coat” — which spends the listing defending against a disadvantage the data says does not exist, and repeats the myth to every reader.

These are real dogs from the dataset who waited a month or more. The app computes each one’s measured disadvantage in SQL, then hands Gemini a brief naming which factor to lead against — and which one to say nothing about.

Method

Every number on this page is computed by SQL over the raw public data, not quoted from anywhere. Austin Animal Center publishes intakes and outcomes as two separate tables with no stay identifier, and a dog can pass through the shelter many times, so joining on animal_id alone produces a cross product. The fix is to number each animal’s intakes and outcomes by date and pair the nth with the nth, which recovers 172,069 distinct stays. Waits are capped at 365 days and the analysis is restricted to 46,749 adoption outcomes for dogs.

Data: Austin Animal Center Intakes and Outcomes, public domain. Analysis: DuckDB. Listing text: Google Gemini. Breed here is the shelter’s own visual identification, which is known to be unreliable for bully breeds — that is a property of the label, and it is the label adopters see too.
